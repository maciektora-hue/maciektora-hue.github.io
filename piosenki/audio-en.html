What is this about?

We took the recordings in the library and treated them as signals rather than as a list of titles, genres or lyrics. So we measure the sound itself: where the energy sits, how much low and high frequency content there is, how the spectrum changes, how concentrated the harmony is, what the attacks look like, how the dynamics behave, what happens in the midrange, and how the stereo field is built.

After cleaning the data, 856 recordings remained. They did not form three airtight boxes. Instead, three clear and stable cores emerged: Depth, Light and Motion, plus bridges, a transition region and a periphery.

What we did

1. We cleaned the audio dataset

Starting point: 891 analysed files. We removed 35 wrong downloads, wrong versions and other collisions. That left 856 recordings for the actual analysis.

2. We measured the sound

For every file we computed a broad set of features: dynamics, spectrum, frequency bands, MFCCs, chroma, onsets, tempo and stereo.

3. We looked for geometry

We did not assume that everything had to belong to one neat cluster. We checked whether stable cores existed and how the rest of the dataset connected to them.

4. Three islands emerged

Depth, Light and Motion are acoustically distinct, but not isolated from one another. The topology of the transitions between them is one of the most interesting parts of the result.

What emerged

Depth 166

Darker, heavier at the bottom, bass-rich and more tonally concentrated. Less bright, with more room for quiet passages and stronger individual attacks.

Light 60

The brightest, most broadband, grainy and airy island. A lot of high-frequency energy and many small spectral changes.

Motion 117

The most midrange-centred and fluid. More harmonic movement, less bass dominance and somewhat wider stereo. It is the main mediator between the two extremes.

The most striking fact: there is almost no direct Depth ↔ Light bridge. These two ends of the landscape usually transition into one another through Motion.

Map of the whole landscape

The islands are only the cores. The rest of the library forms bridges, a transition continent and a periphery. That is why we do not simply call this “three clusters”. For once, the mathematics declines to pretend that everything fits into three shoe boxes.

How we did it, without the pain yet

The audio files were brought into a common analytical form and described using measurable features. We then compared the tracks in several representations and checked whether the same cores reappeared independently of how we looked at the data. They did. The islands are separated most strongly by spectral shape and the distribution of energy; tempo turned out to matter surprisingly little.

We adopted the three-island model because it gives stable, interpretable cores while refusing to pretend that every track must belong to one pure category.

What exactly was measured?

This is not a “lots of bass / little bass” analysis. For every recording we computed a whole set of signal quantities and descriptors derived mathematically from the audio waveform. Some describe amplitude and signal energy directly, some describe how that energy is distributed over frequency, and others describe variation over time, stereo, tonality and rhythm.

Level and dynamics

RMS and level in dBFS, peak values, crest factor, the fraction of samples near or above 0 dBFS, plus the distribution of loudness over time: mean, standard deviation and percentiles.

Frequency spectrum

Spectral centroid, roll-off, bandwidth, spectral flatness, spectral slope, edge frequencies, and energy split into bands from 20 Hz to 20 kHz.

Spectral change over time

Spectral flux, spectral variability and frame-by-frame statistics. This means two tracks with a similar average amount of bass can still differ strongly in how their sound moves over time.

Contrast and timbre

Spectral contrast across multiple frequency ranges, plus 13 MFCC coefficients and their variability. These describe the shape and colour of the spectrum, not merely the total amount of energy.

Harmony and tonality

Twelve chroma components, their distribution and entropy, dominant pitch class, harmonic concentration, tuning, key candidate, major/minor mode and confidence in that estimate.

Attacks and rhythm

Onset strength, count and rate, intervals between attacks, variation in those intervals, several BPM estimates and rhythm-confidence measures.

Stereo

Separate left- and right-channel levels, L/R balance, channel correlation and the Mid/Side relationship, including the width of the stereo image.

Grain and signal “roughness”

Zero-crossing rate and its variability, spectral flatness and several high-frequency descriptors. These help distinguish smoother, more tonal sound from noisier, brighter or grainier sound.

In practice, a single track is therefore not reduced to one number called “energy”. It is represented by a multidimensional profile of level, dynamics, timbre, spectrum, temporal movement, harmony, rhythm and stereo space.
