Sound map: Depth, Light and Motion

An analysis of how the tracks actually sound, independently of lyrics and semantic tags.

→ Main analysis result

The library did not split into three airtight boxes. Instead, three stable sound cores emerged — Depth, Light and Motion — connected by bridges and a broad transition zone.

What is this about?

We treated the recordings in the library as signals rather than as a list of titles, genres or lyrics. The analysis therefore focuses on the sound itself: where the energy sits, how much low- and high-frequency content there is, how the spectrum changes, how concentrated the harmony is, what the attacks look like, how the dynamics behave, what happens in the midrange, and how the stereo field is built.

After cleaning the data, 856 recordings remained. In that space, three clear cores appeared, together with bridges, a transition region and a periphery.

What we did

1. We cleaned the audio

Starting point: 891 analysed files. After removing wrong downloads and incorrect versions, 856 recordings remained for the actual analysis.

2. We measured the sound

For every file we calculated a broad set of features: dynamics, spectrum, frequency bands, MFCCs, chroma, onsets, tempo and stereo.

3. We looked for geometry

We did not assume in advance that every track had to belong to one neat cluster. We tested whether stable cores existed and how the rest of the library connected to them.

4. Three islands emerged

Depth, Light and Motion are acoustically distinct, but not isolated from one another. The topology of the transitions between them is one of the most interesting parts of the result.

What emerged

Depth 166

Darker, heavier at the bottom, bass-rich and more tonally concentrated. Less bright, with more room for quiet passages and stronger individual attacks.

Light 60

The brightest, most broadband, grainy and airy island. It carries a lot of high-frequency energy and many small spectral changes.

Motion 117

The most midrange-centred and fluid. More harmonic movement, less bass dominance and somewhat wider stereo. It is the main mediator between the two extremes.

The most striking fact: there is almost no direct Depth ↔ Light bridge. These two ends of the landscape usually transition into one another through Motion.

How we did it

The audio files were brought into a common analytical form and described using measurable features. We then compared the tracks in several representations and checked whether the same cores reappeared independently of how the data were viewed. They did. The islands are separated most strongly by spectral shape and the distribution of energy; tempo turned out to matter surprisingly little.

The three-island model gives stable, interpretable cores without pretending that every track has to belong to one pure category.

What exactly was measured?

For every recording we calculated a set of signal quantities and descriptors derived mathematically from the audio waveform. Some describe amplitude and signal energy, others the distribution of that energy across frequency, and others variation over time, stereo, tonality and rhythm.

Level and dynamics

RMS and level in dBFS, peak values, crest factor and the distribution of loudness over time.

Frequency spectrum

Spectral centroid, roll-off, bandwidth, spectral flatness, spectral slope and energy split into bands from 20 Hz to 20 kHz.

Spectral change over time

Spectral flux, spectral variability and frame-by-frame statistics.

Contrast and timbre

Spectral contrast across multiple frequency ranges and MFCC coefficients together with their variability.

Harmony and tonality

Chroma, harmonic concentration, tuning, key candidate, major/minor mode and confidence in the estimate.

Attacks and rhythm

Onset strength and rate, intervals between attacks, several BPM estimates and rhythm-confidence measures.

Stereo

Channel levels, L/R balance, channel correlation and the Mid/Side relationship.

Signal grain

Zero-crossing rate, spectral flatness and high-frequency descriptors.
