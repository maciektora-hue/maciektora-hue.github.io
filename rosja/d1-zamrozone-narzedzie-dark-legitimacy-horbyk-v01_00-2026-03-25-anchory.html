Dark legitimacy: What Russia and Ukraine reveal about authoritarianism

Roman Horbyk
Institute of Slavic and Eastern European Studies, University of Zürich, Zurich, Switzerland

Communication and Democracy, DOI: 10.1080/27671127.2026.2641731
Published online: 14 Mar 2026. © 2026 The Author(s). Open Access (CC BY 4.0).

Abstract

This forum contribution introduces "dark legitimacy": a mode of authoritarian legitimacy derived not from affirmative expectations of good governance, but from shared negative expectations about power itself. Emerging from Russian political practice after the collapse of late Soviet legitimacy, dark legitimacy stabilizes rule by converting disapproval into a political resource, rendering critique ineffective. Ukraine's recurrent popular mobilization, by contrast, sustains an affirmative communicative legitimacy, making the Russo-Ukrainian War also a conflict between incompatible logics of legitimation.

Keywords: Legitimacy; power; authoritarianism; Russia; Ukraine

History abounds with "futures past"—prophecies laid to rest by the actual course of events. But there is one that evokes an uncanny feeling today. It comes from Oswald Spengler, who in the second volume of The Decline of the West (Spengler, 1922) suggested that Western civilization moved towards the threshold of Caesarism—"unbridled personal régime"—and predicted that "for us [it] will be reached (and possibly passed) in the next hundred years" (p. 419). That is, by about 2022.

This prophecy is inseparable from Spengler's peculiar perspective. But today, personalist styles of politics are indeed on the rise, visible in the concentration of power around individual leaders, the erosion of institutional constraints, and the personalization of accountability in regimes as diverse as Putin's Russia, Modi's India, Duterte's Philippines, Bolsonaro's Brazil, Erdoğan's Turkey, Orbán's Hungary, and, in a different constitutional context, the United States. Its archaicism may seem out of place in the current society—postindustrial, postmodern, late modern, mediatized—where complex networks obscure and obfuscate the workings of power, concealing it behind a sophisticated façade of legitimation. Power based on governmentality, as conceptualized by Michel Foucault (1997), supersedes power based on coercion.

However, I argue that the resilience of many contemporary authoritarian regimes lies in a distinctive way in which they generate political legitimacy. Rather than relying primarily on affirmative claims about good governance, moral authority, democratic consent, expertise, or charismatic leadership, these regimes stabilize rule by mobilizing widely shared expectations about the inherent corruption, coerciveness, and self-interest of power itself. In this model, legitimacy does not depend on persuading citizens that authority is good, just, or beneficial, but on normalizing the belief that all political rule is fundamentally flawed and that existing arrangements are therefore inevitable, tolerable, or preferable to anarchy. I refer to this mode of legitimation as "dark legitimacy." By lowering the threshold of what counts as an acceptable rule, dark legitimacy can render regimes unusually durable, even when governance is ineffective, corrupt, or openly coercive. In what follows, I develop this argument conceptually and illustrate it through communicative practices and public attitudes in post-Soviet Russia, contrast it with the Ukrainian case where such legitimacy was resisted, and briefly situate it within contemporary debates about democratic erosion and executive personalization in the United States.

An affirmative concept of legitimacy

Political legitimacy is the rightfulness of rule. Authority can rest on coercion, but even nondemocratic states seek legal and popular validation. In the classical legitimacy typology by Max Weber (2020), the three key forms of legitimacy are defined as traditional, reliant on customs and laws; charismatic, resting upon the perceived personal qualities of the leader; and rational-legal, derived from the application of effective policies for the benefit of the governed population.

This remains, in combination with the Weberian definition of power as the monopoly on violence, the most widely known theory of legitimacy, although by far not the only one. What is, however, common to differing conceptions and types of legitimacy is that all of them are founded on an affirmative idea about their sources. Legitimacy may be conferred by benign supreme powers, customs, and laws insofar as they are perceived as valuable and appropriate. It may also take the form of rational-legal legitimacy, when authority is regarded as normatively valid and as producing benefits for the population. Common to these affirmative forms of legitimacy is that they can be lost if the regime does not display expected characteristics.

An important caveat: affirmative legitimacy does not mean "progressive," "good," or "beneficial" government. First, nondemocratic regimes also validate themselves affirmatively despite their obvious oppressiveness. Second, even in democratic contexts, there are multiple shortcomings, failures and oppressions. Across history, such governments were complicit in slavery, genocide, antisemitism, xenophobia, and patriarchal oppression. At best, they selectively served the needs of their citizens while discriminating against non-citizens. At worst, they sought to eliminate the entirety of populations. My point is not that affirmatively derived legitimacy is an ultimate good. Rather, it refers to how legitimacy is defined and recognized. In this understanding, legitimacy is established through the presence of a clearly articulated value, mission, or purpose—such as divine sanction, national destiny, popular sovereignty, progress, order, or prosperity—that the regime claims to embody and realize. Legitimacy, in this sense, rests on what the regime asserts it stands for (but not necessarily what it actually stands for). By contrast, the form of legitimacy examined later in this essay is defined not by the presence of such affirming values, but by their absence: legitimacy is inferred from the claim that no better alternative is possible, that all options are equally corrupt, dangerous, or chaotic, and that the existing order persists not because it fulfills a shared good, but because it is perceived as the least bad option. At its most radical, this negatively inferred legitimacy claims that mismanagement, abuse, corruption, and oppression are the values, mission and purpose that the regime claims to embody and realize; they are recast as the norm that needs to be upheld and preserved.

The affirmative concept of legitimacy is particularly obvious in the twentieth-century models of democratic legitimacy. In John Rawls' normative perspective, it rests upon citizens' positive approval, as political power may only be used in ways that all citizens can reasonably be expected to endorse (Rawls, 1993). From the discursive, proceduralist perspectives of Jürgen Habermas (1996) or Benhabib (1994), democracy is legitimized by fair, inclusive, deliberative processes. This emphasis on consent resonates with Antonio Gramsci's understanding of hegemony, in which political power is stabilized not primarily through coercion but through the active production of consent within civil society (Gramsci, 1971). In this sense, legitimacy rests on the successful articulation of a shared normative and discursive horizon. Taken together, these approaches conceptualize legitimacy as something established through the presence of affirmatively articulated norms, procedures, and collective commitments, rather than inferred from the absence of alternatives or the fear of worse outcomes.

Hannah Arendt distinguishes power from violence and grounds legitimacy in collective action. She argues that power "springs up whenever people get together and act in concert, but it derives its legitimacy from the initial getting together rather than from any action that then may follow" (Arendt, 1972, p. 151). This formulation is important here because it defines legitimacy as originating in a moment of collective affirmation and shared purpose, rather than in outcomes, effectiveness, or coercive capacity. Notably, Arendt denied that totalitarian regimes possess power. For her, power arises from people acting together and sustaining shared meaning, whereas violence and coercion appear precisely when this social foundation disintegrates. Totalitarian regimes, therefore, appear strong because they command extensive instruments of violence, yet this reliance on violence signals not strength but weakness: the absence of power grounded in collective action and consent. Lacking such legitimacy, what these regimes claim as legitimacy is merely its semblance.

Governmentality: Affirmative legitimacy beyond democracy

But not only democratic regimes require legitimacy—autocracies, too, must claim a form of legitimation (or its appearance, according to Arendt). One alternative is to think of the modern state in terms of governmentality, as suggested by Michel Foucault (1997). For him, pre-modern power stemmed from sovereignty defined as "the right to kill or let live" (rather echoing Weber's monopoly on violence). In contrast to overtly coercive sovereign power—often described as the continuation of war by other means—early modern Europe developed the idea of government as the management of populations, resources, and conduct, oriented toward the production of desirable outcomes rather than the mere exercise of force. In Foucault's account, this form of power does not derive its legitimacy from being "rational" in a normative sense, but from the establishment of authoritative discourses of truth, expertise, and normality that render governance productive rather than purely repressive. Sovereignty did not disappear but was palimpsestically displaced by governmentality, remaining available as a latent resource—one that contemporary autocracies can readily reactivate when productive forms of legitimation falter. The historical process of this displacement is governmentality.

What follows from this contrast is that authority, unlike the bottom-up model of legitimacy emphasized by democratic theorists through collective action and deliberation, is increasingly conceived as descending top-down from governmental practices themselves, which function as a self-reinforcing source of legitimacy grounded in productive knowledge and truth claims rather than popular consent. Instead of seeking legitimation in popular will, governmentality derives it from discourses of rational self-knowledge and discipline, self-restraint, and techniques of good government applied to biopower and population management.

Taken together, the models of legitimacy reviewed here—archaic, democratic, and governmental—share a common premise: they rely on a baseline expectation that authority possesses at least some affirmative or redeeming qualities. By contrast, post-Soviet Russia illustrates a form of legitimacy grounded in what I call the presumption of the worst—that is, the deep abiding belief that power is corrupt, self-serving, and coercive, yet nonetheless inevitable and preferable to imagined alternatives. In other words, Russia is ruled by a government that does not need to hide its abuses. The legitimization of the worst is accomplished through a cynical assumption that power is always corrupt and that the enemy that you know is better than the one you do not. This presumption of the worst permeates every aspect of Russian society and explains how the government can get away with terrible violence both at home and abroad.

Legitimacy crisis in the Soviet and post-Soviet context

Traditionally, corruption, mismanagement, and the absence of charisma are understood as factors that undermine legitimacy and render autocratic rule unstable. Yet the durability of many such regimes suggests a different mechanism at work: legitimacy need not rest on affirmative expectations of good governance or moral authority, but can instead be sustained by widespread expectations of corruption, coercion, and limited alternatives.

For example, "inverted legitimacy" has been observed in many African states (Bereketeab, 2023; Henderson, 2015), whereby the regime is legitimized through international acceptance instead of domestic approval. For such a regime, it is often enough to control only the capital. Like some African regimes, modern Russia functions as an extractivist captured state—that is, a political system in which governing elites monopolize state institutions to extract and redistribute revenues from natural resource exports, relying on external markets and partners, including China, to sustain the regime's survival and enrichment. Likewise, major global actors in Washington, Beijing, and Brussels have consistently treated the preservation of the Russian state in its existing form as preferable to the risks associated with its collapse. This fear of disorder confers an inverted form of legitimacy on Putin's regime: it is not supported because it is good or just, but because it is perceived as the least dangerous option. Such external acceptance reinforces what I term dark legitimacy, in which both domestic and international actors come to accept coercive rule not through positive endorsement, but through shared expectations that any alternative would be worse.

To understand the post-Soviet evolution of legitimacy, one has to start with its Soviet-era status. While the violent Soviet regime lacked legitimacy in Arendt's specific sense of power arising from people acting together in concert, it rather effectively validated itself through Marxist-Leninist ideology, as a state allegedly serving the interests of the working class. Rigby (1982) explained this as a new type of legitimacy: goal-rationality, derived teleologically from the declared end-goal (Communism). However, even in the 1920s spectacle blended with the more familiar totalitarian features in "everyone's intimate knowledge of what was required of them in every particular situation, and the ability to demonstrate the desired behavior and discourse" (Horbyk, 2014, para. 7). What is distinctive here is not the prescription of identity as such—common to many authoritarian regimes—but the early routinization of cynical performance detached from belief. From the 1970s and into the 1980s, Soviet life had become increasingly theatrical with widespread jokes and tongue-in-cheek gestures by citizens skeptical about "dear leaders" and "developed socialism." Any remaining belief in the promise or effectiveness of Communist rule had been eroding for decades.

The war in Afghanistan became a turning point: it was costly in lives and resources, widely perceived as unwinnable, and exposed the gap between official rhetoric and political reality. Together with the Chornobyl nuclear power plant disaster, obviously caused by systematic negligence among the top management levels that also tried to cover it up at the expense of ordinary citizens, it made the collapse of goal-rational legitimacy unmistakable. The collapse of Soviet ideological and institutional legitimacy in the late 1980s and early 1990s became a formative and deeply traumatic experience for the post-Soviet ruling elites. It also produced individuals who were torn between ideology as preached from high lecterns and the cynical everyday reality of bribery and black markets (Levina, 2017). Across post-Soviet societies, this collapse produced a period of profound anomie—the disintegration of normative expectations and the widespread experience of legal and social disorder in the 1990s. When an ideology with a powerful totalizing metanarrative collapses, relying on a new ideology is extremely hard. It is difficult to mobilize mass belief around a new affirmative ideology in a society where broad segments of the population have become cynical and no longer expect political doctrines to be sincere or binding. As Marxism-Leninism gave way to a pragmatic blend of neoliberal economic discourse and postmodern 'political technologies'—that is, the strategic manipulation of electoral and democratic procedures—post-Soviet elites confronted a direct problem of legitimacy. Symbolic performance alone was no longer sufficient to stabilize rule. Democratic legitimacy was absent: consent and deliberation were manipulated rather than sought.

Here, a crucial difference arose between Russia and the new nation-states emerging out of the former Soviet republics. Whereas Ukrainian governments could consistently ground their legitimacy in the popular will expressed in the 1991 independence referendum and the subsequent construction of a Ukrainian national political community through consent and deliberation (even when democracy faltered), Russia lacked a comparable nation-building resource. Russian statehood and national identity had already been constituted within the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union, both multinational but Russian-centered projects. Even contemporary nationalist appeals in Russia represent not nation-building but a secondary, neo-imperial rearticulation of an already existing state. An example of this may be found in Russkiy mir. Meaning literally "the Russian world," it is a doctrine promoted by the Russian state that people who speak Russian and identify as Russians, but also Orthodox non-Russian Slavs such as Belarusians and Ukrainians, form a single community that Russia has a right and responsibility to unite and manage politically, even beyond its current state borders.

When Putin embarked on his project of consolidating personalist rule, he could not initially rely on revolutionary charisma, heroic military credentials, or a coherent ideological program. This distinguished him from figures such as General Alexander Lebed, a popular Russian military commander whose combat experience in Afghanistan and Chechnya, reputation for toughness, and militarized masculinity provided a ready-made source of personal charisma and legitimacy. Early characterizations of Putin as an opaque and technocratic figure—famously described by Masha Gessen as "a man without a face"—captured this initial ambiguity rather than his later, carefully constructed cult of leadership. Following the collapse of Marxism-Leninism, no stable or widely credible affirmative ideology comparable in scope or depth was immediately available; instead, ad hoc narratives—anti-terrorism, restored greatness, moral conservatism, and, eventually, Russkiy mir—emerged later as compensatory and situational legitimation strategies.

It is debatable whether the Kremlin has now eventually settled for a coherent ideology, but if anything, Eurasianism may be the best match to bring together in the twenty-first century the Soviet anti-Westernism and the old, solid Russian imperialism. Remarkable is its reliance on Lev Gumilev (1912–1992), a Russian historian and ethnologist, and his idea of passionarity as "a capacity for suffering and self-sacrifice" that outweighs the benefits of technology, knowledge or economy in the fight for world domination (Clover, 2022, p. 2). More specifically, it describes the innate drive of a people or ethnic group to achieve or expand influence—essentially, a collective energy for action and historical change. According to Gumilev, some nations have high passionarity, which fuels their rise, conquest, and cultural achievements, while others have low passionarity and tend to stagnate. Aleksandr Dugin, a contemporary Russian far-right political thinker and conspiracy theorist, draws on Gumilev's ideas to frame Russia as a uniquely energetic civilization with a historical mission, justifying assertive foreign policy and national revival. This intertwines a pseudo-historical concept with modern geopolitics, giving an intellectual veneer to nationalist and imperialist narratives. Aleksandr Dugin's ideas notably incorporate many occult elements that function as inversions of monotheistic theology with a distinct "left-hand path," "Satanist" flavor, revalorizing what is universally evil (war, chaos, destruction) as good. This, together with Dugin's use of the Nazi esoteric "black sun" metaphor, would present a striking resonance with the dark inversion of legitimacy as conceptualized here.

Dark legitimacy: Bad is good

Across social and political divides, Russians have long shared a baseline expectation that government is corrupt, self-serving, and unaccountable—a perception commonly summarized by the phrase voruyut vse ("everyone steals"). This widely shared cynicism does not necessarily produce opposition; rather, it underpins a logic of acceptance in which power is tolerated as inevitable and preferable to imagined alternatives presumed to be more dangerous.

When success at mismanaging domestic institutions is flipped to become a positive value, then it is only logical to see the abuse of international institutions as a manifestation of the same affirmative power. To use the parlance of today, "toxic" domestic behavior aligns with "toxic" behavior internationally. Reclaiming the great power status is famously one aim where the priorities of Putin's regime and many Russians meet. Therefore, chaos becomes the holy Grail of legitimacy if it brings the coveted great power status closer—by likewise spreading chaos throughout the world.

Evidence that can support this perspective comes from various empirical data. Firstly, public opinion polls—though questionable in an authoritarian context—show that support for Russian authorities fluctuates peculiarly. Levada Center data indicate Putin's approval fell before military aggressions (Georgia in 2008, Ukraine in 2014, Ukraine in 2022) and then surged afterwards, a pattern mirrored by the other institutions, whose support was incomparably weaker (Levada Center, n.d.). Before each intervention, public opinion on the country's course approval hovered near 50/50, then soared sharply with each round of aggression. Meanwhile, domestic assessments remain dim: a recent poll reports only 20% see corruption declining, while 47% believe it is impossible to eradicate, slightly outnumbering the 43% with the opposite view (FOM, 2024).

The pattern is a pessimistic baseline with sagging approval that rises after new invasions. This may be due to the rally around the flag effect or people being more afraid to speak out during wartime's more repressive phase—the exact reason does not matter because either way it shows rather low legitimacy in need of boosting. If these (problematic) polls are to be believed, Russians are disgruntled about the domestic situation, unless Russia is fighting a war. Normally, they presume the worst—and still approve it.

Secondly, previous research has struggled to explain the stability of the regime despite the widespread unhappiness it had also documented. Even an incomplete review indicates that legitimacy in post-Soviet Russia remained problematic and different from familiar models. Scholars described public attitudes as "resigned acceptance" (Gel'man, 2010), the absence of rational-legal legitimacy (Hutcheson & Petersson, 2016), and declining legitimacy leading to more nationalism (Brusis, 2016). Holmes (2010) alone remained somewhat positive, particularly noting the high elite legitimacy (or self-legitimation), while admitting popular legitimacy might be in crisis. Sil and Chen (2004) contended that high personal approval ratings or lack of uprisings do not warrant legitimacy, but also highlighted that the widespread distrust of the law, the political system, its justice and overall fitness testifies to "a low and declining level of state legitimacy in post-Soviet Russia" (p. 353). For some, pervasive corruption and a well-developed coercive apparatus serve as indirect indicators of compromised legitimacy (Dogan, 2004). Importantly, the legality of government (lawful accession/power transition) is alone insufficient for legitimacy (Beetham, 1991, p. 18). The widespread mistrust of rules is especially telling: according to Beetham (1991), legitimacy is impossible when rules are systematically violated. In the West, the crisis of normativity was seen as the crisis of legitimacy (Habermas, 1973/1975). In Russia, the absence of systematically employed norms is a sign of a serious deficit of legitimacy—but does not affect the overall approval of Putin and consent to be governed.

Thirdly, useful insights come from qualitative media research, showcasing how Russians distrust the media but still reproduce their messaging. Political communication is a key arena for legitimation as well as, within democracy, for deliberation; however, in Russia, deliberation is subverted through manipulation. Alyukov (2022) documented how Russians expressed "negative attitudes towards politics" as "the dirtiest business in the world" (p. 346). The same participants who stated they felt manipulated by official propaganda also repeated its messages. They could still point out "the inadequacy of the administration in dealing with the city's problems" and ridicule "the difference between what participants see on television and experiences from real life" (pp. 347–348), while also universalizing this mismatch: "When one of the focus group participants said 'our state is corrupt,' a government supporter retorted with 'bribes are everywhere'" (p. 351).

In this way, bad governance is reframed not as delegitimizing but as the only possibility, and in this way, an instrument of legitimacy. This is a remarkable departure from any known legitimacy models. Deliberation is not fully absent; rather, it is manipulated into leading to a conclusion that bad governance is good—and must be consented to.

"Bad governance is good" sounds Orwellian—but it also subverts the democratic models and the Foucauldian governmentality. It may preserve its elements as an outer façade in a technologically late modern society but inside that casing the self-replicating legitimacy machine is dead—there is no believable discourse on rationality, governance techniques and skills to squeeze that legitimacy from. It is not unlike the automaton invoked by Walter Benjamin: it seems to be playing chess automatically, but inside is a person moving figures on the chessboard. The name of this person is "sovereignty," and it wields coercive, physical power as a weapon.

This is the essence of dark legitimacy. It can be defined as a form of political legitimacy grounded not in affirmative beliefs about good governance or moral authority, but in shared negative expectations about power itself. It rests on the presumption that political authority is inherently corrupt, coercive, and self-serving, and derives stability from the perception that such behavior is normal, inevitable, or preferable to imagined alternatives. While it may initially operate through defining legitimacy negatively—as the absence of even worse outcomes—it can extend to the active normalization and valorization of coercion, bad governance, and social anomie.

Russia-Ukraine: The war over legitimacy

There is also a contrasting case: Ukraine. Above, I have already mentioned its key difference from Russia in legitimizing through nation-building. Ukraine's elites never participated in Russia's post-Soviet experiment in dark legitimacy. From the late Soviet period onward, political authority in Ukraine was instead grounded in a plural and evolving set of sources, but consistently invoked some version of the "national idea." This idea re-emerged already in the late 1980s through Narodnyi Rukh (the People's Movement of Ukraine), a broad civic and political movement led by dissidents such as Vyacheslav Chornovil and Levko Lukyanenko, who had spent years in Soviet prisons and labor camps for human rights activism and advocacy of Ukrainian sovereignty. Their moral authority and mass mobilization played a decisive role in delegitimizing Soviet rule and articulating Ukraine as a distinct political community.

Importantly, even Soviet-era elites were compelled to accommodate this framework, to which Ukrainians largely consented (as obvious from a whopping 92% support for independence at the 1991 referendum, the literal "act of people coming together" as if taken straight from Arendt). The first two presidents of independent Ukraine, Leonid Kravchuk, a former Communist Party ideologue, and Leonid Kuchma, a representative of the Soviet industrial "red directors," grounded their post-independence authority in appeals to Ukrainian statehood, sovereignty, and development, despite very different governing styles and varying democratic quality. Late Kuchma particularly legitimized his turn to authoritarian governance as rozbudova derzhavy ("state-building") for the common national good. This was still affirmative, not dark legitimacy, although the regime was far from democratic. Such a trajectory culminated with Viktor Yushchenko, who represented a new, non-Soviet political generation and rearticulated the national idea in democratic terms, recasting Ukraine as a sovereign, European, and prosperous polity. Across these successive governments, the national idea functioned as an affirmatively articulated source of consensual legitimacy, sharply distinguishing Ukraine's post-Soviet path from Russia's reliance on negative expectations of power.

Since independence, Ukraine's political legitimacy has also largely rested on democratic procedures, civil society participation, and periodic leadership turnover, rather than on coercion, fear, or external acquiescence. Even during periods of internal instability, Ukrainian institutions and society actively rejected authoritarian shortcuts, demonstrating a collective commitment to democratic norms and legitimacy achieved through deliberation and consent. One such period was the Orange Revolution: mass peaceful protests against electoral fraud in the 2004 presidential election, leading to a court-ordered revote. It symbolized Ukraine's turn to democracy and the rule of law, regionally associated with European institutions and the human rights and liberties tradition.

Another such period came in 2013–2014 with the Euromaidan protests that emerged in response to the government's rejection of a planned and popular association agreement with the EU and escalated into a broader movement against corruption and authoritarianism. At that time, Ukraine was closest to embracing the Russian model of dark legitimacy. Despite Kuchma's earlier authoritarian tendencies, it was only under the presidency of Viktor Yanukovych (2010–2013) that his clan attempted to import dark legitimacy. Intensifying resource extraction for the "Family" and incremental surrender of sovereignty triggered a series of protests, known as maidans (literally, "city squares," a word of Middle Eastern, ultimately Persian origin). They were fragmented, focusing on isolated policy areas, such as Russian bases in Crimea, language policy, entrepreneurs' rights, and taxation. Their cumulative delegitimation of the government culminated in Euromaidan in late 2013, when Ukrainians protested against a U-turn of the government policy, without any sort of debate, from pursuing European integration in favor of integrating with Russia (under Russian pressure).

When Yanukovych tried to crush the protests using Russian coercive-sovereignty tactics (physical dispersal, beatings, kidnappings and murders by riot police), he sought to rewrite Ukraine's social contract by replacing its flawed, although still democratic and affirmatively formulated legitimacy with Russian-style dark legitimacy. He quite literally wanted Ukrainians to presume the worst and still consent to his rule, as it was in Russia. Of course, once again, affirmative legitimacy should not be lionized per se—political systems built on it are still imperfect; however, dark legitimacy is radically different in its destruction of the preconditions for democratization. Ukrainian society rejected this attempt; since then, Russia has tried to impose it militarily through illegal occupation, annexation and genocidal ethnic cleansing. It was, in fact, unsurprising. Euromaidan—also called "The Revolution of Dignity," reflected the protesters' emphasis on personal dignity, civic rights, and resistance to corrupt and humiliating forms of rule. This alone was enough to set the Putinist system of negative values, rooted in radical-evil dark legitimacy, on a direct collision course with Ukraine's positive-value system embodied in its "European choice:" rule of law, human rights, liberty, and justice. The analysis of contemporary media discourses on Europe corroborates that while Ukraine associated Europe with these idealistic values, in Russia it connoted mainly a source of status, technologies, and wealth (Horbyk, 2017). But deep at its heart, it was the conflict between the affirmative legitimacy of Arendtian people power and the dark legitimacy engineered top-down.

This contrast became only more graphic during the war. Even in wartime, Ukrainians deliberated and fought to preserve the architecture of their democracy: from July 22, 2025, Kyiv, Lviv, Odesa, Dnipro, Kharkiv and other cities saw the so-called "Cardboard protests" against Bill No. 12,414, which had stripped the National Anti-Corruption Bureau of Ukraine (NABU) and the Specialized Anti-Corruption Prosecutor's Office (SAPO) of their independence. These were the first anti-government demonstrations since the start of Russia's full-scale invasion, successfully forcing the adoption of a corrective law restoring institutional autonomy on July 31, 2025. At the very same time, Russian forces were conducting what is often termed a "human safari": since May 2024, systematic drone attacks in Kherson deliberately hunted civilians—at bus stops, in cars, near hospitals—killing hundreds and wounding thousands, practices later documented by UN investigators and Human Rights Watch as war crimes and crimes against humanity.

Together, these parallel developments sharply illustrate the divergence between a society that publicly resists the erosion of accountability, even under existential threat, and a regime in which extreme violence against civilians is normalized, celebrated, and legitimized through the logic of inevitability and terror. However, Ukraine's democracy remains under extreme stress due to martial law and the ongoing war pressures. The conflict's prolongation increases the risk that Ukrainian society could gradually slip into dark legitimacy, too, as cynicism, anger, and despair take hold—a dynamic that may align with one of Putin's strategic objectives of fostering a "forever war" environment.

Consequences of dark legitimacy

Dark legitimacy has several consequences. First, it makes defeating the regime incredibly hard. This is the primary reason for its invention. If the political process may be imagined indeed as a swinging pendulum cycle, from one party to another (or between clans/influence groups in a nondemocratic context), then what post-Soviet Russian elites achieved was the full stop of the pendulum in the lowest point of expectations—after the collapse of Soviet legitimacy, they discovered that the lowest point is always in equilibrium.

The Russian opposition has dedicated tremendous effort to unmask the rot of corruption and abuse, for example, thanks to Aleksey Navalny's movement and Anti-Corruption Foundation (FBK). However, under the regime of dark legitimacy it has borne no fruit. Firstly, everybody—even regime supporters—already presume the worst. Secondly, they accept that the worst is the natural or even optimal condition (for Russia or even universally). Thirdly, they reject the possibility of improvement as naïve, preferring the stability of the equilibrium in its lowest point. The regime may even favor and foster such "anti-corruption" work to maintain cynicism and curb the most damaging excesses.

As a result, it does not work at all to attack a dark legitimacy regime as incompetent, corrupt, inefficient, or even evil, hoping to shake its foundations—it only reinforces them, for they are precisely incompetence, corruption, inefficiency and malice. Its only pillar is the archaic sovereignty—pure coercive power—and it possibly only fears being seen as weak. This points to a potential alternative but any particular recommendations for oppositional communicative strategy remain premature before more work is done on analyzing dark legitimacy regimes.

The second major consequence is that dark legitimacy not only supports bad governance, it also renders the idea of good governance illegitimate. Just as post-Soviet liberalization reforms (and their key figures, including Gorbachev himself) remain intensely hated in Russia, any idea of reform, progressivism or a mere improvement is dismissed out of hand by passive and active supporters of the regime with a nod to the failed 1990s experiments.

The temptation of dark legitimacy is not confined to authoritarian regimes. As Salek (2023) and Nasi (2025) noted, political communication in Europe and North America increasingly exhibits symptoms of democratic malaise: declining trust and institutional fatigue. In such contexts, legitimacy risks shifting from consent and accountability toward resignation and cynicism while expectations of good governance diminish—a dynamic that is particularly relevant for the United States, where democratic infrastructure persists but affirmative presumptions are eroding. European democracies are not immune to this in any way, either. Dark legitimacy, once normalized, hollows democracy from within: it does not abolish elections or constitutions, but renders them symbolically irrelevant.

From this perspective, support for Ukraine is not merely geopolitical or moral. It is democratic self-defense. Ukraine's resistance demonstrates that legitimacy can still be grounded in dignity, accountability, and collective agency, while Russia's model shows how power can stabilize itself through negative expectations alone. Confronting dark legitimacy abroad is therefore essential to preventing its diffusion at home—and to averting the late-modern Caesarism Spengler once predicted.
